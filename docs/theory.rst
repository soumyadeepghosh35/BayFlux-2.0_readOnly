Method
======

This page describes what BayFlux computes and how, and points at the modules
that implement each piece. For the full treatment see the paper.

The problem
-----------

Metabolic fluxes cannot be measured directly. Exchange fluxes at the cell
boundary can, by watching extracellular concentrations change over time, but
intracellular fluxes have to be inferred: a reaction can carry enormous flux and
still leave almost no trace in metabolite pools, because whatever it produces is
consumed just as fast.

Two things constrain them. Conservation of mass at steady state gives

.. math::

   S v = 0

where :math:`S` is the stoichiometric matrix over :math:`m` metabolites and
:math:`n` reactions, and :math:`v \in \mathbb{R}^n` is the flux vector. And
:sup:`13`\ C labeling experiments give mass isotopomer distributions: feed the
organism a substrate labeled on specific atoms, and different flux profiles
shuffle those atoms differently, producing measurably different labeling
patterns downstream.

For a genome-scale model the system is severely underdetermined. Roughly 3000
fluxes are constrained by one to five exchange measurements and around 60
metabolite measurements. Many flux profiles fit the data equally well, and the
traditional response, reporting the single best fit with a confidence interval,
represents that badly, especially when the region of good fit is not a single
connected blob.

The Bayesian formulation
------------------------

BayFlux instead characterises the fluxes probabilistically, seeking the
posterior

.. math::

   p(v \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid v)\, p(v)}{p(y)}

Measurements are assumed independent with Gaussian error,

.. math::

   y_k = M(v) + \varepsilon_k, \qquad \varepsilon_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_k^2)

where :math:`M(v)` simulates the mass isotopomer distributions for a flux vector.
The likelihood is therefore

.. math::

   p(y \mid v) = \prod_k \frac{1}{\sigma_k \sqrt{2\pi}}
       \exp\left\{-\frac{(y_k - M(v))^2}{2\sigma_k^2}\right\}

The prior is uniform on the polytope defined by :math:`S v = 0` together with
the flux bounds. Exchange fluxes measured accurately enter as hard bounds,
narrowing the polytope; if they are uncertain they can be moved into the
likelihood instead.

The marginal likelihood :math:`p(y)` is intractable in this many dimensions, so
the posterior is only available up to a constant. That is exactly the situation
MCMC is for.

In the code: :py:func:`bayflux.core.likelihood.normpdf` evaluates one Gaussian
log-density and :py:func:`bayflux.core.likelihood.logLikelihood` sums it over
every measured EMU, calling the EMU simulation for :math:`M(v)`. ``averageError``
in the configuration is :math:`\sigma`.

Simulating labeling: the EMU method
-----------------------------------

:math:`M(v)` has to be evaluated once per proposal, millions of times per run,
so it dominates the cost. BayFlux uses the Elementary Metabolite Unit framework
of Antoniewicz et al., which decomposes the labeling problem into the smallest
subsets of atoms whose labeling state must be tracked, then solves a small
linear system per EMU size.

Three things make the implementation fast enough:

* The repetitive matrix work is done in Fortran, through LAPACK and BLAS. That
  is the ``emusFortran`` extension module, built from
  ``bayflux/core/emusFortran.f90``.
* **EMU pruning** collapses long branch-free pathways into a single EMU
  transition, without loss of information.
* **Transition merging** identifies functionally equivalent atom transitions,
  for instance from parallel reactions with identical mappings, and merges them.

The decomposition is compiled once, ahead of sampling, into a list of matrix
operations; :py:func:`bayflux.core.emus.emuCompile` does that and
:py:func:`bayflux.core.emus.simulateLabeling` executes it for a given flux
vector. Pruning and merging are optional arguments to ``emuCompile``.

The EMU network is built only for what is needed to predict the measured data.
Extra metabolites can be requested at compile time, which is how you cross
validate a model by holding a measurement out, or generate synthetic data for
experimental design.

Sampling: the AcMet algorithm
-----------------------------

Proposals are generated by the Artificial centering Metropolis algorithm,
AcMet, which adapts Artificial Centering Hit-and-Run to Bayesian inference.

ACHR is the standard way to draw pseudo-uniform samples from a flux polytope. It
maintains a running average of the samples as a *center*, and moves by picking a
random *edge point*, heading from the center towards it, and drawing uniformly
along the segment inside the polytope. Two properties made it unsuitable as it
stood.

First, ACHR updates the center at every step. A moving center makes the sampler
non-reversible, which destroys the MCMC guarantee. Second, ACHR samples net
fluxes only, but cyclic fluxes, meaning simultaneous forward and reverse flux
through one reversible reaction, are irrelevant to stoichiometry and decisive for
labeling.

AcMet fixes both:

* **Two phases.** Collect uniform samples with ordinary ACHR until the center
  stops moving, then lock it and discard those samples. With the center fixed the
  chain is reversible and Metropolis acceptance is valid. This is what
  ``centeringSamplesPerTask`` controls, and what ``findCenter`` does standalone,
  writing a drift trace so you can confirm the center has settled.
* **Extra edge points.** For every reversible reaction two more edge points are
  added, one maximising forward while minimising reverse flux and one the other
  way round, so the sampler can explore cyclic fluxes as well as net fluxes.

A proposal :math:`v'` is accepted with the Metropolis probability

.. math::

   \alpha = \min\left\{1, \frac{p(v' \mid y)}{p(v \mid y)}\right\}
          = \min\left\{1, \frac{p(y \mid v')}{p(y \mid v)}\right\}

The normalising constant cancels, and so does the prior, because it is uniform.

:py:class:`bayflux.core.sampler.MCMCACHRSampler` implements this, extending
COBRApy's ``HRSampler``. Passing a ``likelihood`` function to
:py:meth:`~bayflux.core.sampler.MCMCACHRSampler.sample` locks the center and
switches on Metropolis acceptance; calling it without one runs the centering
phase.

The multi-proposal extension
----------------------------

BayFlux 2.0 adds a generalized Metropolis-Hastings sampler that evaluates
several proposals per iteration rather than one, which is what makes the
parallel executable worth running.

Each iteration generates ``numProposals`` proposals, one per worker process,
scores them in parallel, and assembles a transition matrix over the resulting
states from their pairwise likelihood ratios. Because the proposal distribution
is symmetric, the acceptance ratio reduces to the likelihood ratio alone. The
steady-state distribution of that matrix is then computed by power iteration, and
``subsamplesPerIteration`` states are drawn from it.

The modules involved:

:py:mod:`bayflux.core.transitionProbabilities`
   Builds the transition matrix, with bounds on the log-likelihood ratio for
   numerical stability and a rescaling step for rows whose off-diagonal entries
   saturate.

:py:mod:`bayflux.core.steadyState`
   Power iteration with damping for the stationary distribution.

:py:mod:`bayflux.core.worker`
   The worker process body. Attaches to shared memory, generates a proposal,
   scores it, writes the result back.

:py:mod:`bayflux.core.acceptanceRate`
   The acceptance rate diagnostic. Rates below about 0.2 mean proposals are too
   aggressive, above 0.5 too conservative; roughly 0.234 is optimal in high
   dimensions.

Convergence
-----------

Convergence is assessed with the Gelman-Rubin statistic :math:`\hat{r}` across
four independent chains, comparing within-chain and between-chain variance. A
run is treated as converged when at least 80 % of the reactions with non-zero
sampling variance reach :math:`\hat{r} < 1.10`. Reactions fully fixed by
stoichiometry have no variance and are excluded.

The number of samples required scales approximately linearly with the number of
reactions carrying variance. See :doc:`configuration` for sizing.

Terminology
-----------

Edge point
   A point on the boundary of the feasible flux polytope, used by ACHR as a
   direction reference. Typically the maximum and minimum flux for each
   reaction, found by linear optimization. The ACHR literature calls these
   "warm-up points"; BayFlux says "edge point" to avoid collision with the MCMC
   sense of warm-up.

Warm-up samples
   Samples from the start of an MCMC run, before the chain has reached its
   stationary distribution. Normally discarded.

Cyclic flux
   Simultaneous forward and reverse flux through one reversible reaction,
   quantified as forward minus reverse. The :sup:`13`\ C MFA literature
   sometimes calls this an "exchange flux"; BayFlux reserves that term for
   extracellular exchange.

Extracellular exchange flux
   Flux of a reaction that moves a metabolite across the cell boundary, such as
   nutrient uptake. Usually measurable directly from concentration changes over
   time.
